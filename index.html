Problem-solving

Case studies

Real data problems: question, data, method, result, lesson.

Case 1: Making the quick stats tool handle messy input

Question: What should the tool do when a visitor types numbers in different formats, or types something that is not a number?

Data: Numbers typed or pasted by a visitor, separated by commas, spaces, semicolons or new lines.

Method: The tool splits the text on those separators, ignores empty pieces, converts each piece to a number, and checks all of them before it calculates anything.

Result and lesson: If any entry is not a number, the tool shows the first few bad entries instead of a wrong answer. Lesson: check the data before calculating, because one bad value can spoil every result.

Case 2: Cleaning a messy ticket export

Question: Can the raw ticket export be trusted before any analysis is done?

Data: A raw file of 123 sample ticket rows with duplicates, mixed spelling and two date formats.

Method: A Python script removed duplicates, fixed spelling and spacing, converted dates to one format, and replaced category and technician names with ids.

Result and lesson: 3 duplicates were removed, 25 rows of spelling and spacing and 18 rows of dates were fixed, leaving 120 clean rows, and no ticket was closed before it was created. Lesson: clean and check the data first, because the same category written three ways would be counted as three categories.

Case 3: Checking the SQL results against the Excel dashboard

Question: Do the SQL results and the Excel dashboard tell the same story?

Data: The results of six SQL queries and the totals in the Excel dashboard.

Method: Each query result and chart total was compared with the dashboard, and six data quality checks were run, such as row counts, duplicate ticket numbers and consistent category names.

Result and lesson: All six checks passed and the totals match. Lesson: check results a second way before reporting them, so the findings can be trusted.

Ticket Analytics Dashboard

Project overview

This project analyzes IT support tickets. A raw, messy export is cleaned, loaded into a database, and questioned with SQL. The answers are shown in charts and an Excel dashboard: which problems happen most, how long they take to fix, and when requests peak, so an IT team can plan its work.

Problem statement

IT teams receive many tickets but rarely study the pattern. Without analysis, repeat problems stay unfixed and staff are not scheduled for the busiest days.

Objectives

  • Clean and organize the ticket data.
  • Answer four questions with SQL: what, how long, when and who.
  • Build charts and an Excel dashboard.
  • Write findings and recommendations.

Target users

An IT manager who plans staffing and priorities, and help desk technicians who want to find and fix repeat problems.

Data cleaning

From the raw file (123 rows) to the clean file (120 rows):

  • 3 duplicate rows removed.
  • 25 rows with extra spaces or mixed spelling fixed, such as "network", "NETWORK" and " Network ".
  • 18 rows with a second date format converted to one format.
  • Checked that closed_at is after created_at: 0 problems.
  • Category and technician names replaced with ids.

Data model (ERD)

SQL queries

-- 1. Tickets per category
SELECT c.name AS category, COUNT(*) AS total
FROM tickets t JOIN categories c ON t.category_id = c.id
GROUP BY c.name ORDER BY total DESC;
-- 2. Average hours to resolve, per category
SELECT c.name AS category,
       ROUND(AVG(TIMESTAMPDIFF(HOUR, t.created_at, t.closed_at)), 1) AS avg_hours
FROM tickets t JOIN categories c ON t.category_id = c.id
WHERE t.closed_at IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY c.name ORDER BY avg_hours DESC;
-- 3. Tickets per weekday
SELECT DAYNAME(created_at) AS weekday, COUNT(*) AS total
FROM tickets GROUP BY weekday ORDER BY total DESC;
-- 4. Tickets per day
SELECT DATE(created_at) AS day, COUNT(*) AS total
FROM tickets GROUP BY day ORDER BY day;
-- 5. Repeat problems
SELECT title, COUNT(*) AS times
FROM tickets GROUP BY title HAVING COUNT(*) > 1 ORDER BY times DESC;
-- 6. Technician workload
SELECT te.name AS technician, COUNT(*) AS tickets
FROM tickets t JOIN technicians te ON t.technician_id = te.id
GROUP BY te.name ORDER BY tickets DESC;

Every query result was compared with the totals in the Excel dashboard. They match.

Findings

On the sample data, Network is the biggest category with 38 of 120 tickets (32%), followed by Hardware (27) and Software (24). Hardware tickets take the longest to resolve, 8.6 hours on average, and Account tickets are the fastest at 1.8 hours. Monday is the busiest weekday with 29 tickets, and weekends are quiet (10 tickets in total). The most repeated problems are "Cannot reach shared folder" and "Cannot connect to Wi-Fi", with 10 tickets each. Because this is sample data, these findings show how the analysis works. They are not real results.

Recommendations

Based on the sample data: put the most effort into network problems, which are about a third of all tickets, and fix the repeat causes first, such as shared folder access and Wi-Fi connection. Look at why Hardware tickets take about 8.6 hours to fix, for example by keeping common spare parts ready. Schedule more technicians on Mondays, the busiest day, and fewer on weekends.

Limitations

The data is a small sample: 120 made-up tickets over 8 weeks, all of them closed, so the findings only show how the analysis works. SQLite suits this small data set and would be replaced by MySQL for larger data.

Future improvements

Reflection

My journey so far.

I am Fritz Gerard Mallari, a college student at Datamex College of Saint Adeline. I started college in 2023, after finishing senior high school at AMA Computer Learning Center. Building this portfolio is part of my journey toward becoming a junior data analyst.

Before I started learning about data, I thought numbers were only something to calculate and hand in. Now I understand that numbers are only useful when someone explains what they mean. A table of values does not help a team until the data is cleaned, checked, and turned into a clear answer. That idea is why my tagline says that good data should tell a clear story.

The most important lesson for me so far is that good analysis starts with a good question. Before opening any tool, I need to know what I want to find out, where the data comes from, and who will use the result. I also learned that clean data matters as much as clever methods. If the data is messy, even a simple average can mislead people, so checking and cleaning come first.

Making this website taught me how to organize my work so other people can understand it. I wrote the quick stats tool so that a visitor can type in numbers and instantly see the mean, median, mode, spread, and a chart. Building something people can try showed me that explaining a result clearly is a skill of its own, not only the calculation.

My biggest challenge is that I am still new and do not yet have many finished projects to show. I handle this by being honest about my level. I call myself a junior analyst because that is where I am. I do not want to claim skills that I cannot prove, so every skill on this site is meant to point to real work. As I finish projects, I will add them here one by one.

There is still a lot I need to improve. I want to get more comfortable with spreadsheets and basic statistics, practice explaining my findings in plain language, and learn tools that analysts use every day. I also want to build the habit of documenting each project, including the question, the data, the method, and the result, so that someone else can follow what I did.

My strengths are the ones I can already rely on. I work hard, I stay flexible, I am dedicated, I adapt to new situations, and I can keep working under pressure. I am also willing to be trained, which I believe is the best quality a beginner can bring to a team. I am ready to learn from experienced people and to accept feedback.

The analyst I want to become is someone who is dependable and clear. I want to be the person who turns messy numbers into answers that a team can trust and use. My goal is to join a team where I can gain hands-on experience, take on new challenges, and contribute to its success.

I am ready for this next step because I have the attitude, the discipline, and the willingness to learn. I may be at the start of my career, but I am serious about growing in it, and I will keep improving this portfolio as I grow.
